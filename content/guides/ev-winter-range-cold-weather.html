The first cold snap of EV ownership is unsettling. A car that comfortably did 240 miles in September shows 170 in January, and nothing has changed except the weather.

Nothing is wrong. Cold weather reduces the usable range of every electric car, typically by around 20 to 30% in British winter conditions, occasionally more in a hard freeze. Petrol cars lose economy in the cold too — it is simply less visible because the tank is refilled in three minutes.

The useful part is that the loss has two separate causes with two different fixes, and one of them is largely recoverable.

Where the range actually goes

Cabin heating is the larger cause. A petrol engine is so inefficient that it produces waste heat in abundance, and warming the cabin is essentially free. An electric motor is efficient enough that there is not enough waste heat to use, so the car has to generate heat deliberately — and in a car with a resistive heater that can draw several kilowatts continuously. On a short urban journey, heating can consume a substantial fraction of the energy the car uses.

Cars fitted with a heat pump do much better here, moving heat rather than making it, often for a third of the energy. If you are choosing an EV and live somewhere cold, a heat pump is one of the few options with a genuinely measurable everyday benefit.

Battery chemistry is the smaller cause. Cold lithium-ion cells have higher internal resistance and deliver less usable capacity. Some of this returns as the pack warms through driving. It is a real loss, but less of the total than most people assume.

Cold batteries also charge slowly

This catches people out more than the range drop does. A cold battery will not accept high DC charging rates, and the management system will limit the session hard to protect the cells. Arrive at a 150kW charger with a battery that has been sitting outside overnight and driven ten minutes, and you may see a small fraction of the rate you expect.

The fix is preconditioning. Most modern EVs will warm the battery on the move when you set a rapid charger as the navigation destination in the car's own system. The car uses some energy doing it, and gets far more back in charging speed. On a winter trip this is worth ten to fifteen minutes per stop and it is the single highest-value habit in this guide.

The catch: on most cars this only triggers via the built-in navigation, not your phone. In winter, route to the charger in the car.

What actually helps

Precondition the cabin while plugged in. Almost every EV lets you warm the car on a timer or from an app before you leave. Do it while still connected to the charger and the energy comes from the grid rather than the battery — so you start with a warm car, a warmed pack and a full state of charge. This is the closest thing to a free lunch in EV ownership and a large share of owners never set it up.

Use the heated seats and steering wheel instead of the cabin heater. Heating a person directly takes a fraction of the energy of heating the air around them. Seat heating draws tens of watts; cabin heating draws thousands. Turn the cabin heat down a couple of degrees and the seats up, and you will not feel colder.

Park somewhere sheltered when you can. A garage, or even a lee side out of the wind, leaves the pack meaningfully warmer overnight.

Keep the state of charge higher than you would in summer. Not because the battery needs it, but because your buffer against a slow charging session or an unexpected detour should be larger when everything takes longer.

Check your tyre pressures. Pressure falls with temperature, and underinflated tyres cost range in any car. EVs are heavy, which makes this worth more than it is on a petrol hatchback.

Slow down slightly. Aerodynamic losses dominate at motorway speed and cold air is denser. The penalty for 75mph is steeper in January than in July.

Planning winter journeys differently

Take whatever range you plan around in summer and cut it by roughly a quarter. A route you did with one stop in August may need two in January, and pretending otherwise is how people end up crawling along the inside lane with the heating off.

Shorten your legs, keep the same 20% arrival buffer, and precondition before every rapid stop. Bear in mind that charging itself is slower in the cold, so budget longer per stop as well as more of them.

This is not battery damage

Worth stating plainly, because it worries people: winter range loss is temporary and entirely reversible. The capacity comes back with the temperature. It is not degradation, it is not a fault, and it does not accumulate year on year.

Long-term battery degradation is a separate and much slower phenomenon — modern packs typically retain the large majority of their capacity after many years and high mileage, helped by avoiding routinely charging to 100% and by not relying exclusively on rapid charging. Neither of those has anything to do with what you see each February.

Your car is fine. It is just cold, and so are you — which is why the seat heater is the right answer.
