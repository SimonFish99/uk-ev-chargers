The UK's Charging Networks, Compared

Who runs the chargers you will actually meet — InstaVolt, Gridserve, BP Pulse, Osprey, Ionity, Tesla and the rest — and what each is genuinely good and bad at.

Written by the PlugMap team  ·  Last reviewed September 2026  ·  9 min read

On this page

  1. The rapid and ultra-rapid networks
  2. Destination and slower networks
  3. On-street charging for drivers without a driveway
  4. Roaming, apps and the one card that works everywhere
  5. How to choose in practice

There is no single UK charging network. There are dozens of operators running overlapping estates with different hardware, different pricing, different apps and markedly different reliability records. Knowing who runs what turns an anxious detour into a routine stop.

What follows is a practical characterisation of the operators you are most likely to encounter, based on what each is known for rather than on a price table that would be out of date within a month. Every town page on PlugMap lists the operator for each charge point, so you can check who you will be dealing with before you commit to the drive.

The rapid and ultra-rapid networks

InstaVolt has built a strong reputation among drivers for one specific reason: contactless-first, no app required, no account, just tap and charge. Sites are typically at retail parks, garden centres and roadside restaurants rather than motorway services. Reliability is generally well regarded. If you value not downloading anything, this is the network to look for.

Gridserve operates the Electric Highway, which includes a large share of motorway service area charging, along with purpose-built Electric Forecourts — sites designed from scratch around charging rather than retrofitted into a petrol station. The motorway estate has been through a substantial upgrade programme from the ageing hardware it inherited, and the newer bays are a different experience from the old ones.

BP Pulse runs one of the largest and oldest networks in the country, which cuts both ways: excellent coverage including plenty of units in places nobody else has bothered with, alongside a legacy estate of older hardware of varying condition. Increasingly integrated into BP forecourts. A subscription tier exists and can be worth it for heavy users of this network specifically.

Osprey focuses on high-power hubs with several bays per site, which matters more than it sounds — a site with six chargers is a fundamentally different proposition from a site with one, because one broken unit is an inconvenience rather than a crisis. Contactless throughout.

Ionity is a joint venture between several major carmakers, built around very high power sites on major routes across Europe, including the UK. Excellent hardware, positioned squarely at long-distance drivers. Pricing sits at the premium end unless you have a manufacturer tariff through your car brand, which several partners offer and which changes the value proposition completely.

Fastned brings its distinctive yellow canopies from the Netherlands, with a growing UK presence. Reliable hardware and a clear, well-designed app. Canopies are an underrated feature in a British winter.

Tesla Supercharger remains the benchmark for reliability and ease of use, and a substantial and growing share of UK sites is now open to non-Tesla drivers. Non-Tesla users pay more than Tesla owners do unless they take a membership, and access is via the Tesla app. If you drive something else and have dismissed Superchargers, it is worth re-checking which sites near your routes have opened up.

MFG EV Power and Applegreen Electric have both built out rapid hubs on forecourt sites, often with decent facilities attached — a real consideration when you are going to be standing around for twenty minutes.

Destination and slower networks

Pod Point is heavily represented in supermarket and retail car parks, including a long-running partnership with a major grocery chain. Many bays are 7kW AC, some are rapid, and a number remain free to use while you shop — genuinely good value for a weekly top-up.

Shell Recharge covers both forecourt rapids and a wider estate absorbed through acquisition, so hardware varies by site.

ChargePlace Scotland is the publicly supported Scottish network, with coverage reaching well into rural and Highland areas that commercial operators have not prioritised. If you are driving in the north of Scotland, this network is often the answer.

Source London operates a large on-street estate in the capital, aimed squarely at residents without driveways. Membership tiers affect the rate significantly.

On-street charging for drivers without a driveway

Roughly a third of UK households have no off-street parking, and a distinct category of operator exists to serve them.

ubitricity converts existing street lampposts into charge points — minimal street clutter, low installation cost, typically around 3 to 5kW. Slow, but if it is outside your house and you are asleep, slow is irrelevant.

Char.gy works similarly with lamppost and bollard units, often via local authority partnerships.

Connected Kerb installs kerbside sockets designed to be upgraded over time, frequently through council schemes.

The common thread is that these are residential overnight charging, not journey charging. If you have no driveway, the realistic strategy is a nearby on-street unit for routine charging plus occasional rapid stops, and it is worth checking what exists on your own street before assuming an EV is impractical.

Roaming, apps and the one card that works everywhere

Roaming services such as Zap-Pay, Octopus Electroverse and Bonnet aggregate multiple networks behind a single account and payment method. The appeal is obvious: one app, one bill, no forest of accounts.

The catch is that coverage varies, not every network participates, and rates through a roaming provider are sometimes marginally above the operator's direct price. They are a convenience purchase. Since contactless became mandatory on new units of 8kW and above and on existing 50kW+ rapids, the case for them is weaker than it was — though for frequent drivers the consolidated billing is still worth something.

How to choose in practice

For most people the honest advice is to stop optimising. Work out which one or two networks dominate the routes you actually drive, get their apps, and use contactless for everything else.

Three things matter more than the brand on the unit. How many bays are on the site — a six-bay hub is far more dependable than a single unit however good the operator. Whether it is a newer installation — most operators' recent hardware is better than their old hardware. And what is next to it — twenty minutes is much more pleasant beside a coffee shop than in an empty retail park at the back of a Homebase.
